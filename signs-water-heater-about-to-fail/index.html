5 Signs Your Water Heater Is About To Fail

By the Pottstown Express Plumbing team · Plumbing Tips

Have Questions?

Call (610) 672-7601

Most water heaters don't fail without warning. They give a homeowner several weeks or even months of subtle signals before the actual failure, a full loss of hot water or, worse, a tank that ruptures and floods a basement or utility closet. Here are the five signs worth taking seriously the first time you notice them, not after the third time.

1. Rust-Colored Water

Rusty, discolored, or rust-colored water, especially if it clears up after running the tap for a minute or two, usually points to internal tank corrosion rather than anything in your home's supply lines. This happens once the anode rod has been fully consumed and the tank itself has started corroding. Once you're seeing rust consistently, the tank's structural integrity is already compromised, even if it's still technically holding water for now.

A quick way to narrow it down: rust from the hot tap only, with clear water from cold, points specifically to the water heater. Rust from both hot and cold suggests the issue is somewhere in your home's supply piping generally, a different and often less urgent problem than a failing tank.

2. Popping Or Rumbling Sounds

A sound like popcorn popping or gravel rumbling inside the tank during heating is sediment at the bottom of the tank trapping small pockets of water that boil and burst through the buildup. It's not dangerous in the moment, but it's a clear sign the tank has accumulated enough sediment to meaningfully reduce efficiency and, over time, stress the tank from the inside.

This sound often starts subtly and gets more noticeable over months as sediment continues to build. Once it's loud enough to hear from another room, the buildup has typically been accumulating for a long time, meaning the efficiency loss has likely been happening just as long, even if the water heater otherwise seems to be working fine.

3. Water Taking Longer To Heat, Or Running Out Faster

A gradual decline in how much hot water you get before it turns lukewarm, or how long it takes to heat back up between uses, usually means the heating element or burner is working against a growing sediment layer rather than heating the tank directly. This tends to develop slowly enough that people adjust their habits around it before ever connecting it to the water heater itself.

A useful test: if a tank that used to comfortably supply back-to-back showers now leaves the second person with noticeably cooler water than it did a year ago, and nothing else in the household's usage has changed, that decline itself is meaningful data, not just an inconvenient coincidence.

4. Any Moisture Around The Base Of The Tank

This is the sign to stop and act on immediately rather than monitor. Moisture at the base can mean a failing pressure relief valve, a loose fitting, or, most seriously, a hairline crack in the tank itself from internal corrosion. A tank that's actively leaking from its shell rather than a fitting is at real risk of a larger rupture, and that's a genuine water damage scenario, not just an inconvenience.

The temperature and pressure relief valve, required on every water heater under the International Plumbing Code (IPC), is designed to release pressure safely if the tank overheats or over-pressurizes. If you find that valve dripping regularly rather than during an occasional test, that's the system telling you something upstream, often sediment-related overheating, is already off from normal operation.

What Doesn't Necessarily Mean Failure

Not every unusual sound or symptom means the tank is dying. A single pop when the heater first kicks on, condensation on a cold water pipe nearby, or a brief drip right after installation as fittings settle are all generally normal and don't belong on this list. The signs above are specifically about persistent, worsening patterns over weeks or months, not one-off occurrences you notice once and never see again.

5. The Water Heater Is Simply Old

Age alone is a legitimate warning sign. A tank unit past 8 to 10 years old in our area, given Pottstown's moderate water hardness, is statistically much more likely to fail soon than a newer unit, even if it hasn't shown any of the other four signs yet. The data plate on the side of the tank lists the manufacture date if you're not sure how old yours actually is.

An old unit that's still running fine today isn't a reason to ignore its age. Tank failures often happen suddenly once a unit crosses this age threshold, even after years of apparently normal operation, which is exactly why age itself belongs on this list alongside symptoms you can actually see or hear.

Acting on an early sign, rather than waiting for a full failure, usually means the difference between a scheduled repair appointment and an emergency call after hours, or worse, a tank that has already started leaking into a finished basement or a utility closet full of stored belongings and important documents.

The Bottom Line

A water heater rarely fails completely out of nowhere. Catching one or two of these signs early is usually the difference between a scheduled repair or replacement and an emergency call after a tank has already started leaking into your basement or utility closet.
