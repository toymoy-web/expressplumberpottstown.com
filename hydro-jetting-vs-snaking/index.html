Hydro Jetting Vs. Snaking: Which Does Your Drain Actually Need?

By the Pottstown Express Plumbing team · Plumbing Tips

Have Questions?
(610) 672-7601
Call (610) 672-7601

Snaking, or cabling, is the method most people picture when they think of drain cleaning, and it's genuinely the right tool for most clogs. Hydro jetting is the more intensive alternative, and knowing which situation actually calls for it can save you money on jobs that don't need it while pointing you toward it on the ones that genuinely do need the more thorough approach.

How Snaking Actually Works

A cable auger is fed into the drain and either cuts through or hooks and pulls out whatever's blocking the line, a hair clog, a foreign object, a localized buildup. It's fast, effective for the immediate blockage, and the lower-cost option for a straightforward, isolated clog. Its real limitation is that it clears a path through the obstruction without addressing buildup along the rest of the pipe's interior.

How Hydro Jetting Actually Works

A jetting nozzle delivers a high-pressure water stream through the line, and depending on the nozzle style, either cuts forward through a blockage first or scours the pipe's interior surface on a rotating or flailing pattern as it's pulled back through. The result is a genuinely clean pipe wall, not just a cleared center channel, which is the core, meaningful difference between the two methods.

When Snaking Is Actually The Right Call

A single, isolated clog, a hair clog in a bathroom drain, a foreign object stuck in a toilet trap, a first-time slow drain with no history of repeat problems, is a good match for cabling. There's no reason to pay for the more intensive jetting service on a problem that a simple cable pass will resolve completely. Most residential drain calls fall into this category.

When Jetting Is Actually Worth It

A drain that clogs repeatedly despite being cabled multiple times is the clearest sign cabling alone isn't addressing the real problem, accumulated buildup along the pipe walls that a cable simply pushes past rather than removes. A kitchen line with a known history of grease buildup, a sewer lateral with confirmed root intrusion, and a main line serving a home with older cast iron or clay piping are all situations where jetting's thorough cleaning genuinely outperforms another routine cabling.

Commercial kitchens are a particularly strong case for jetting on a preventive schedule rather than waiting for a reactive clog, since ongoing grease accumulation there is predictable and recurring, exactly the kind of buildup jetting is designed to remove.

A main line that serves multiple fixtures, rather than a single isolated drain, is also worth extra consideration for jetting when it clogs, since a blockage there affects the whole house rather than one sink or tub, and the consequences of an incomplete fix are correspondingly larger.

What A Camera Inspection Actually Shows

Beyond just confirming whether jetting is appropriate, a camera inspection reveals the specific nature of what's inside the pipe: root intrusion at a particular joint, a bellied section holding standing water, grease buildup along a specific length of line, or simply confirmation that the pipe interior is clean and the clog was an isolated foreign object. That specificity is what turns a generic recommendation into an actual, evidence-based diagnosis rather than a guess.

The Camera Inspection Step Most People Skip

A camera inspection before choosing a method tells you what's actually happening inside the pipe, whether it's a simple, removable blockage a cable will handle fine, or genuine buildup and root intrusion that calls for jetting. Skipping this step and defaulting straight to the more expensive jetting service on every call wastes money on jobs that didn't need it; defaulting to cabling on every call means paying for repeat visits on a problem jetting would have solved the first time.

Is Jetting Ever Risky?

On pipe that's already significantly corroded, cracked, or otherwise structurally compromised, high-pressure jetting can, in rare cases, cause further damage to an already weak section. This is exactly why a camera inspection beforehand matters: confirming the pipe is structurally sound before jetting protects against turning a cleaning job into an emergency repair. On pipe confirmed to be in reasonable condition, jetting is safe, standard practice used across residential and commercial plumbing nationwide, not an unusual or experimental technique.

Cost Comparison, Realistically

Cabling is consistently the lower-cost option for a single visit, which is appropriate given it's solving a narrower problem. Jetting costs more per visit but, on a line that genuinely needs it, tends to last considerably longer before the same clog returns, which can make it the more cost-effective choice over time despite the higher upfront cost for the right situation.

A useful way to think about the comparison: paying for three separate cabling visits over a year on a line that genuinely needed jetting from the start often costs more in total than one jetting service would have, on top of the ongoing inconvenience of the drain backing up repeatedly in between visits.

Getting The Right Call For Your Specific Drain

Our drain cleaning & unclogging page covers our standard cabling service for the majority of residential clogs, and our hydro jetting page covers when we recommend the more intensive approach and why, including the camera inspection step that informs that recommendation before any work begins.

The Bottom Line

Most clogs genuinely need nothing more than a standard cable, and there's no benefit to paying for jetting on a problem that doesn't call for it. A drain with a real history of repeat clogs, grease buildup, or root intrusion is a different story, and on that specific kind of problem, jetting solves what repeated cabling only manages temporarily, which matters most on the pipes carrying the highest cost of a repeat failure, whether that cost is measured in dollars, downtime, or property damage.
