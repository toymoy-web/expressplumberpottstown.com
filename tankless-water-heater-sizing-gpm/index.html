How Many Gallons Per Minute Do You Actually Need From A Tankless Water Heater?

By the Pottstown Express Plumbing team · Plumbing Tips

Have Questions?

Call (610) 672-7601

If you've started shopping for a tankless water heater, you've probably run into GPM, gallons per minute, as the number every sizing guide leads with. That's the right number to focus on, but most generic sizing charts are built around national averages that don't actually apply to a home in Pennsylvania. Here's what the number really means and how to size it correctly for your household.

Forget Tank Capacity, Think Flow Rate Instead

A traditional tank water heater is rated by how many gallons it stores. A tankless unit doesn't store anything, it heats water instantly as it flows through, so its rating is a flow rate: how many gallons per minute it can heat to your target temperature at once. Think of a tank as a swimming pool you draw down until it's empty, and a tankless unit as a powerful hose that just keeps flowing as long as you need it.

Why Pennsylvania's Groundwater Temperature Actually Matters

Most generic sizing guides assume incoming water arrives around 50 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit, which understates the job for our area. Pennsylvania's average groundwater temperature runs closer to 47 degrees, meaning a tankless unit here has to raise the water temperature further to hit a comfortable 120 degrees at the tap than the same unit would in a warmer climate. A sizing calculation that ignores this can leave you with a unit that technically meets the "average" household number but still runs cold during a real Pennsylvania winter.

The relevant number here is called temperature rise, the gap between your incoming water temperature and your target output temperature. A calculator that assumes 60-degree incoming water and calculates a 60-degree rise to reach 120 will undersize a unit for our actual 47-degree groundwater, which needs a 73-degree rise to hit that same target. That 13-degree difference in required rise sounds small on paper, but it directly reduces the maximum flow rate a given unit can deliver at full capacity, since every tankless unit's GPM rating drops as the required temperature rise increases. A unit rated for 9 GPM at a 60-degree rise might only deliver 7 GPM at the 73-degree rise Pennsylvania winters actually demand.

This is also why manufacturer spec sheets typically show multiple GPM ratings tied to different temperature rises, and why the number printed largest on a retail box isn't always the number that applies to your actual installation. A unit shopped online off a headline GPM figure without checking the corresponding temperature rise is a common way homeowners end up disappointed with a unit that "should" have been big enough.

Winter Changes The Math More Than People Expect

Groundwater temperature isn't static throughout the year, and shallow water lines feeding a home can run even colder than the regional average during a hard winter stretch, particularly in older Pottstown-area homes where supply lines weren't always buried to modern frost-line depth standards. That means the coldest weeks of the year are exactly when a marginally-sized tankless unit is most likely to underperform, delivering less hot water flow at precisely the moment demand for long showers and extra laundry loads tends to go up.

Add Up What You Actually Run At Once

Sizing starts with your real peak demand, not a household headcount. A standard shower runs roughly 2 to 2.5 gallons per minute. A kitchen faucet running full is closer to 1.5. If two people might shower at the same time while a dishwasher runs, you're looking at a combined demand in the 6 to 7 GPM range, not the 3 to 4 GPM a single-shower estimate would suggest.

Most residential gas tankless units fall in the 7 to 9 GPM range, enough for a typical household running two to three fixtures simultaneously. Larger homes with more bathrooms sometimes need two units working together rather than one oversized unit trying to cover everything.

Undersizing Is The Most Common Mistake

Homeowners frequently size to their average day rather than their peak moment, the one time a week when three showers and a load of laundry all happen within the same ten minutes. A tankless unit sized for your average use will disappoint you at exactly the moment you notice it most. There's no real benefit to oversizing dramatically either, but undersizing to save a few hundred dollars up front is the more common and more frustrating mistake.

The fix for a genuinely large household isn't always a single bigger unit either. Past a certain point, running two smaller tankless units in parallel, each handling a portion of the home's fixtures, delivers more reliable performance than pushing one large unit to its absolute maximum rated flow continuously. This is a real installation consideration worth discussing on larger homes rather than defaulting to the single biggest unit on the shelf.

Getting A Real Number Instead Of An Estimate

An accurate sizing conversation covers three things: your home's realistic peak simultaneous fixture use, your incoming water temperature based on your actual water source, and your existing gas line's capacity to support the unit you're considering. Skipping any one of these and relying on a generic online calculator is how a homeowner ends up with a tankless unit that technically matches the national average household but underperforms for their specific home and climate.

Where Water Heater Repair And Tankless Sizing Overlap

If you're weighing a switch from a struggling tank unit, start with whether a repair still makes sense, or whether it's time to size a replacement properly. For the tankless-specific installation and sizing conversation itself, our tankless water heater service page covers what a proper on-site sizing assessment actually involves, checking your real fixture count, your gas line capacity, and your household's actual peak usage pattern before recommending a specific unit.

The Bottom Line

A tankless water heater sized correctly for Pennsylvania's colder incoming water and your household's actual peak demand delivers exactly what it promises: continuous hot water without running cold at the worst possible moment. A unit sized off a generic national chart often doesn't. If you're not sure which category your household falls into, a real on-site assessment beats an online calculator every time.
