What To Do In The First Hour After A Basement Flood

By the Pottstown Express Plumbing team · Plumbing Tips

Have Questions?
(610) 672-7601
Call (610) 672-7601

The first hour after discovering a flooded basement determines a lot about how the next few weeks go. Acting correctly, and in the right order, limits both the damage and the cost, while the wrong instinct, even a well-meaning one, can make things worse or put you at real risk. Here's what actually matters in that first hour.

Safety Comes Before Anything Else

If there's any standing water near an electrical outlet, a panel, or any plugged-in appliance, do not enter that area. Water and electricity are a genuinely dangerous combination, and no amount of stuff you're trying to save is worth the risk. If you can safely reach your home's main electrical panel from dry ground, shutting off power to the affected area is the right first move. If reaching it means walking through standing water to get there, don't. Wait for a professional or your utility company instead.

The same caution applies to gas appliances in a flooded area. If a water heater or furnace has been submerged or is sitting in standing water, don't attempt to operate it, and don't assume it's safe just because it looks fine from the outside.

If anyone in the household uses a basement bedroom or spends significant time in the space regularly, account for their safety specifically. A flooded basement with compromised electrical safety isn't a space to re-enter casually even after the immediate flooding appears to have stopped, since standing water can mask a live electrical hazard that isn't obvious from a quick look.

The Standard Every Restoration Company Follows, Even If They Don't Say So

Professional water damage restoration follows the IICRC S500 standard, the industry reference for water damage restoration that defines how extraction, drying, and moisture monitoring should actually be done to confirm a structure is genuinely dry, not just dry to the touch. Knowing this standard exists is useful even before you call anyone, since it tells you what a proper response should actually look like: real equipment, real moisture monitoring, and verification before the job is considered finished.

Stop The Source If You Can Do It Safely

If the flooding is coming from a specific, identifiable source, a burst pipe, a failed appliance connection, a sump pump that's stopped working, shutting off that source is the single most useful thing you can do in the first few minutes. For a burst pipe, that means the main water shutoff. For a failed appliance, the appliance's own shutoff valve. If the water is coming from outside, from flooding or a storm event rather than an internal failure, there may be nothing to shut off, and that's a different situation requiring extraction rather than a source fix.

Document Before You Touch Anything

Once it's safe to be in the space, take photos and video of the water level, the affected areas, and any damaged belongings before you start moving anything or beginning cleanup. This documentation is what your insurance claim will actually rely on, and it's far easier to capture in the first hour than to reconstruct later once items have already been moved or the water has receded and the true extent is less obvious.

Photograph water lines on walls, the specific items affected, and anything that shows how high the water reached at its worst point. If you have a moisture meter or even just a tape measure, noting the actual water depth adds useful detail to your documentation.

Move What You Can, Prioritizing Value And Vulnerability

Once you've documented the scene, move anything still above the waterline but at risk, boxes, furniture, electronics, up onto higher shelving or out of the basement entirely if that's practical. Prioritize based on both monetary and sentimental value and how vulnerable an item is to water damage; paper documents and photos are often the most urgent since they can be destroyed within hours of sustained moisture exposure, while some furniture can tolerate more time.

Don't Wait To See If It Dries On Its Own

Mold growth can begin within 24 to 48 hours in wet drywall, insulation, and flooring. A household dehumidifier and a few fans can help manage humidity in a room that's damp but not actively flooded, but they won't dry out a genuinely saturated structure the way commercial extraction and drying equipment does. Waiting to see if it dries out on its own is one of the most common reasons a manageable water event turns into a much larger mold remediation problem days later.

If the situation has progressed beyond active extraction into needing structural drying, repair, and a longer restoration process, our this page on the fuller restoration process covers that in more detail.

What Not To Do

Don't run electrical equipment, including a shop vac, in standing water without confirming power is safely off first. Don't assume a small amount of water isn't worth addressing quickly, since even modest flooding left unaddressed for days can develop the same mold risk as a larger event. And don't wait for insurance approval before starting basic safety steps or documentation, those are yours to handle immediately regardless of what your policy ultimately covers.

The Bottom Line

The first hour is about safety first, then stopping the source if possible, then documentation, then getting help moving quickly rather than waiting to see how bad it gets. Acting in that order, rather than skipping straight to cleanup, protects both your safety and your insurance claim, and it's the difference between a contained situation and one that spreads.
