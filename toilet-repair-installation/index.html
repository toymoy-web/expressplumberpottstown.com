The Most Common Toilet Problems We See

A running toilet is almost always a worn flapper or fill valve, an inexpensive fix that still wastes real money on the water bill if left alone. A toilet that rocks slightly or leaks at the base often means the wax ring has failed, which left unaddressed can damage the subfloor underneath. A weak or incomplete flush can point to a partial clog needing a drain cleaning visit, a low water level in the tank, or, on an older unit, a design that was never a strong flusher to begin with.

Older toilets in Pottstown's mid-century homes, and the similar-era housing common in Royersford, sometimes use a flush mechanism and tank design that hasn't been manufactured in decades, which means a broken part occasionally has to be sourced rather than picked up off a shelf. We'll tell you upfront if a repair part needs to be ordered instead of assuming a same-day fix.

Repair Or Replace

Most running toilets and weak flushes are a straightforward parts repair. A cracked tank or bowl, a failed wax ring on an older unit already due for other repairs, or simply wanting a more water-efficient model are the usual reasons to replace rather than repair.

New toilet installations fall under the International Plumbing Code's water-efficiency requirements, which is why current units are built around a 1.6 gallon or lower flush compared to older 3.5 to 5 gallon toilets common in Pottstown's mid-century homes. A straight swap from an old unit to a modern one can noticeably change household water use over a year.

What A Failed Wax Ring Actually Means

The wax ring creates the seal between the toilet base and the drain flange below it. Once that seal breaks, water and sewer gas can seep out with every flush, usually slowly enough that it isn't obvious until the subfloor around the base has already started to soften. Catching it at the rocking-toilet stage, before real subfloor damage sets in, keeps this a same-visit repair instead of a floor repair project.

A flange that's cracked or sitting below the finished floor level, common after a flooring upgrade that raised the floor height without adjusting the flange, is a related problem that looks like a wax ring failure but needs a different fix. We check the flange itself, not just replace the wax ring, whenever a toilet has failed at the base more than once.

Installing A New Toilet The Right Way

A new installation isn't just setting a toilet on a ring and bolting it down. We check that the flange is at the correct height, the closet bolts are properly seated, and the supply line and shutoff valve are in good working condition before calling the job finished, since skipping any of these is exactly how a brand-new toilet ends up leaking within the first year.

What To Do If A Toilet Is Overflowing

Shut off the supply valve

The valve is usually on the wall behind the toilet, low to the floor. Turn it clockwise to stop water flow.

Remove the tank lid and lift the float

If you can't find the shutoff quickly, lifting the fill valve float manually stops the tank from refilling.

Don't keep flushing

Repeated flushing on a clog risks an overflow onto the floor.

Have a towel ready

A small amount of overflow is common; contain it before it reaches flooring seams.

Frequently Asked Questions

My toilet runs constantly. Is that a big repair?

Usually not. A worn flapper or fill valve is a quick, inexpensive fix in most cases.

The toilet rocks slightly when I sit down. Is that serious?

It can be. A rocking toilet often means the wax ring seal has failed, and continued use can damage the subfloor. Worth addressing promptly.

Do you install customer-supplied toilets?

Yes, we can install a toilet you've purchased, or help you choose one during the visit.

How long does a full toilet replacement take?

Most installations are completed within an hour or two, assuming no flange or subfloor repair is needed underneath.
